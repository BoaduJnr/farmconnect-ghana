FarmConnect Ghana · Project Documentation

1. Introduction & Background

Ghana has over 2.7 million smallholder farmers, most of whom farm fewer than 2 hectares and sell into informal, fragmented supply chains with little price transparency. FarmConnect Ghana is a mobile-first web platform built to close that gap directly: it lets a farmer publish what they have to sell, lets a buyer find it and pay for it, and wraps that core transaction in the supporting infrastructure a real rural market needs — live price information, an AI-powered agricultural advisor, a feature-phone SMS channel for users without a smartphone, and cooperative/trust/dispute-resolution mechanisms.

This project was undertaken as the CSCD602 Advanced Software Engineering capstone, carried through the complete software engineering lifecycle — requirements engineering, system analysis and design, implementation, testing, deployment, and documentation — by Group Angry Nerds, University of Ghana, Department of Computer Science. It is not a mock-up: every screen, endpoint, and workflow described in this documentation set exists in running, tested code (see the Testing Report) and is reachable at the live application URL in Links.txt/README.

2. Problem Statement

  • No direct market access. Smallholder farmers typically sell through middlemen who, by the project's own market research (cited in the original SRS), capture up to 60% of the potential profit margin.
  • No price transparency. Without a reliable, current view of commodity prices, farmers cannot tell whether an offered price is fair, weakening their negotiating position further.
  • Post-harvest loss. An estimated 30–40% of harvested produce spoils before it reaches a buyer, a direct consequence of slow, informal, geographically limited sales channels.
  • Digital exclusion. Existing digital agriculture platforms are largely designed for smartphone-first, English-only, high-connectivity users — excluding exactly the population (feature-phone users, Twi speakers, low-end Android devices, intermittent 3G) this project targets.
  • No trusted way to transact remotely. A farmer and a buyer who have never met have no shared trust signal and no safe way to hand over money for produce that hasn't been seen yet — a real barrier to any farmer-to-buyer digital marketplace in this context, not a hypothetical one.

3. Aim & Objectives

Aim: to design, build, test, and deploy a complete, functional, low-cost digital marketplace that connects Ghanaian smallholder farmers directly to buyers, market price information, and agricultural advisory services — accessible on both smartphones and basic feature phones.

Objectives

  1. Enable farmers to list produce (crop, quantity, price, harvest date, photos, location) and buyers to discover and order it, without a middleman.
  2. Provide a safe, zero-custody payment hand-off via Mobile Money, with a human-verifiable confirmation step and an administrator-arbitrated dispute path.
  3. Surface live, per-crop market prices so farmers can negotiate from a position of information, not guesswork.
  4. Provide an AI-powered advisory channel for crop, pest, and weather guidance, including photo-based pest identification.
  5. Support users with no smartphone or data plan via a parallel SMS command channel covering every critical action.
  6. Build trust between strangers transacting for the first time via a mutual rating system and an administrator moderation/dispute layer.
  7. Deliver the system bilingually (English/Twi) and accessibly, and deploy it to a publicly reachable, live URL.

5. Requirements Gathering & Analysis

Requirements were gathered by combining (a) published statistics on Ghana's smallholder-farming sector and known post-harvest-loss/middleman-margin figures (cited in SRS §1.4), (b) domain reasoning about the concrete constraints of the target user (feature phones, intermittent connectivity, Twi as a first language for many farmers, no bank/card relationship but near-universal Mobile Money access), and (c) iterative refinement of an AI-assisted first draft (disclosed in full in SRS Appendix A) into a structured IEEE-830 specification, which the team then reviewed against what could realistically be built, tested, and deployed within a single-semester capstone.

The single most important analytical step taken after v1.0 of the SRS was written was reconciling it against implementation reality once real engineering constraints appeared — most significantly, that this team could not obtain production Mobile Money merchant/PSP API access, and that no public Ghana Commodity Exchange API exists to integrate with. Rather than silently drop or quietly reinterpret those requirements, every such gap was analysed, a concrete alternative was designed, and the decision and its rationale were written back into the SRS as a dedicated reconciliation section (SRS §9) — the analytical output of this project's requirements work is therefore the full v1.0→v2.0 diff, not just the final list of what got built.

11.2 What was actually built

All 7 planned implementation phases are complete: (0) scaffolding, (1) phone+OTP auth with role selection, (2) listings + marketplace search, (3) orders + manual Mobile Money reconciliation, (4) AI advisory chatbot with photo pest-ID, (5) live price feed + notifications + SMS fallback, (6) co-ops + admin/moderation + ratings, (7) PWA offline caching + full bilingual pass + accessibility pass. This is not a partial or prototype build — every use case in §7 is backed by working, tested code (Testing Report §3/§5).

11.3 Code organisation

The backend is organised into 11 domain modules (auth, users, listings, orders, prices, advisory, notifications, co-ops, admin, sms-gateway, uploads), each with its own routes/controller/service files, sharing common middleware (requireAuth, requireRole, validateBody/Query) and a single Prisma client. The frontend mirrors this with a page-per-screen structure and a thin, centralised API client that handles token refresh transparently. Both apps depend on packages/shared for every enum and Zod validation schema, so the two apps cannot silently drift apart on what a valid request/role/status looks like.

11.4 Error handling & validation

Every request body/query is validated against a Zod schema before it reaches business logic (400 on failure, with field-level error detail); every domain error (e.g. "not your listing," "listing already pending," "momo not set up") is a typed error class mapped to the correct HTTP status by a single handleKnownErrors helper, rather than ad hoc try/catch scattered per route.

16. Security Considerations

AreaPosition taken
AuthenticationPhone + OTP (no passwords to leak or reuse); OTP hashed at rest (bcrypt) with a 5-minute TTL and single-use deletion; 20/hour/phone rate limit against abuse.
Session managementShort-lived (12h) JWT access tokens; 30-day refresh tokens that are single-use and rotate on every refresh via a Redis allowlist — logout genuinely revokes a session, which a bare stateless JWT cannot do on its own.
AuthorisationEvery route explicitly declares its required role (requireRole); ownership checks (e.g. "is this your listing/order?") are enforced in the service layer, not assumed from the UI. Verified by manual probing in Testing Report §4.
Payment securityThe platform never stores or transmits a Mobile Money PIN, card number, or wallet credential — by construction, not by policy (SRS §9.1/NFR-S3).
Input validationEvery request body/query validated against a Zod schema before touching business logic (SRS NFR-S2-adjacent, demonstrated in Testing Report §4 test 11).
Known residual riskAccess/refresh tokens are stored in browser localStorage, not httpOnly cookies — a documented XSS-exposure trade-off (SRS §9, Design Documentation "cross-cutting concerns") with a scoped remediation plan in Maintenance_and_Evolution.pdf §5.
Regulatory postureBy never taking custody of funds, FarmConnect avoids acting as a payment service provider under Bank of Ghana e-money rules (SRS §6.1) — a deliberate design choice, not an oversight.

17. Challenges & Solutions

ChallengeSolution adopted
No realistic path to production Mobile Money merchant/PSP API access within a single-semester capstone.Replaced automated gateway escrow with a manual, human-verified reconciliation workflow that never takes custody of funds — safer regulatory posture, achievable in the available time (SRS §9.1).
No public, developer-accessible Ghana Commodity Exchange price API exists.Built a self-contained, realistically-behaving simulated price feed behind a thin service boundary, so a real integration can be swapped in later without touching the UI or the rest of the API (SRS §9.2).
The SRS gave ratings, co-ops, and admin/moderation one sentence of intent each, with no workflow or data model.Designed each from scratch during implementation, with the reasoning for every non-obvious choice (one co-op per farmer, leadership succession rule, no self-service admin signup) recorded in Design_Documentation.pdf §11, not left implicit in code.
Serving both smartphone and feature-phone users from one system.A parallel SMS command grammar (§Testing Report UC coverage; User_Manual.pdf §5) covering every critical action, sharing the same underlying data model and business logic as the app.
Verifying the system actually works, not just believing it does.Layered testing strategy (unit → integration against a live database → manual smoke tests against the actually-running server) — see Testing_Report.pdf §1, and the deliberate choice to run tests against real Postgres/Redis rather than mocks, since several real defect classes only appear against real database/cache behaviour.

18. Limitations

Stated plainly, as good engineering practice requires:

  • Payments are a manual reconciliation process, not an automated escrow — trust and diligence from both parties still matter, backstopped by (but not eliminated by) the dispute-resolution workflow.
  • Market prices are a realistic simulation, not sourced from a real exchange, because no such public API exists to integrate with.
  • NFR-P2 (10,000 concurrent users) and NFR-R1 (99.5% uptime) are architecturally plausible but not empirically load-tested at this submission's scale (Testing Report §11).
  • Frontend automated test coverage is thin relative to the backend (2 component test files versus 14 integration test files) — the backend carries essentially all business logic, but page-level UI regression coverage is a known gap (Testing Report §11).
  • The EXTENSION (Agricultural Extension Officer) role named in the original SRS is reserved in the data model but has no implemented screens yet.
  • Render's free-tier web service sleeps after ~15 minutes idle, adding a ~30–50s cold-start delay to the first request after a period of inactivity (Deployment_Guide.pdf) — acceptable for grading/demo use, not for a real production pilot at scale.

20. Future System Evolution / Enhancements

See Maintenance_and_Evolution.pdf §7–§10 for the full roadmap. In brief, five prioritised, independently-shippable future phases: (8) hardening & observability — close the open performance finding, broaden frontend test coverage, add real load testing; (9) real-time & push — replace REST polling with WebSocket/Web Push; (10) logistics & delivery tracking — the domain explicitly excluded from v1 scope; (11) an optional, feature-flagged licensed Mobile Money aggregator for users/regions that outgrow manual reconciliation; (12) activating the reserved Extension Officer role for agricultural staff and NGO partners. AI/emerging-technology enhancements are scoped as extensions of the capability already in production (Gemini + weather context + price history), not speculative new dependencies — proactive weather-risk alerts, price-trend summarisation, a listing-photo-quality assistant, admin fraud-signal ranking, and voice input for search.

21. Conclusion

FarmConnect Ghana set out to connect Ghanaian smallholder farmers directly to buyers, fair price information, and agricultural advisory services, in a form that genuinely fits its target users — including those on feature phones with no data plan. The delivered system meets that aim: every functional requirement in the SRS is either implemented as specified or implemented via a documented, justified deviation; every core use case is covered by both automated tests against a live database and manual verification against the actually-running server; the application is deployed to a live, publicly reachable URL on infrastructure chosen specifically to remain free and accessible through grading and beyond; and every simplification, trade-off, and open item is written down rather than glossed over, with a concrete plan for what comes next. The gap between "software requirements specification" and "shipped, working system" is where most real-world engineering difficulty lives — this project's documentation set is built to make that gap, and how it was closed, fully visible to an examiner.
